# Solar Tropical Cycle

## Overview

The **solar tropical cycle** describes the motion of the Sun along the ecliptic
as observed from Earth, relative to the **Earth’s axial tilt**.

It is the cycle that defines:
- the progression of seasons,
- changes in daylight length,
- climatic rhythms,
- and the structure of the civil calendar year.

The tropical cycle is anchored to **solar declination**, not to distance or orbital speed.
In ChronoCompass, it is treated as a **seasonal and illumination cycle**, focused on geometry and light rather than temperature or weather.

Month-based labels (March, June, etc.) are used as current-era civil references. ChronoCompass treats equinoxes and solstices as the primary anchors; month names are secondary and may change over very long timescales.

---

## Cycle Definition

- Average duration: **~365.24219 days**
- Cycle type: **Sun–Earth seasonal geometry**
- Reference frame: Earth’s equatorial plane relative to the ecliptic

Astronomically, the tropical cycle runs from **March equinox to March equinox**.
In the solar tropical wheel, this astronomical convention naturally совпides with the ChronoCompass model:
**East (E)** is anchored to the March equinox and therefore serves as both the physical and formal start and end of the cycle.

---

## Directions and Anchors

The solar tropical wheel uses the following primary anchors:

| Direction | Astronomical Meaning |
|-----------|----------------------|
| **E**     | March Equinox        |
| **N**     | June Solstice        |
| **W**     | September Equinox    |
| **S**     | December Solstice    |
| **E+**    | Next March Equinox   |

These anchors are defined **astronomically** and are independent of hemisphere.

> Note:  
> While cultural interpretations of these dates differ between hemispheres,
> their astronomical meaning is global and unambiguous.

---

## Houses

- The wheel is divided into **16 houses**, centered on the 16 directions.
- Houses represent **states of solar declination and illumination**, not weather seasons.
- Temporal lengths of houses are close to equal, but not perfectly identical due to orbital mechanics.

Houses describe how the Sun’s apparent path and daylight geometry evolve over the year.

---

## Meaning of Directions

### East (E) — March Equinox

- The Sun crosses the celestial equator moving northward.
- Day and night are approximately equal worldwide.

East represents:
- **initiation of the tropical cycle**,
- geometric balance,
- the transition from decreasing to increasing solar declination,
- the canonical beginning and completion of the cycle in ChronoCompass.

---

### North (N) — June Solstice

- Maximum northern solar declination.
- One hemisphere experiences maximum daylight duration,
  while the other experiences minimum daylight duration.

From a geometric perspective:

- The Sun reaches its maximum angular separation from the equatorial plane.
- Solar illumination is maximally asymmetric between hemispheres.

North represents:
- **maximum expression of solar declination**,
- peak illumination contrast,
- a stable extreme of the cycle.

---

### West (W) — September Equinox

- The Sun crosses the celestial equator moving southward.
- Day and night are again approximately equal worldwide.

West represents:
- completion of the high-declination phase,
- redistribution of solar illumination,
- transition toward decreasing declination.

---

### South (S) — December Solstice

- Maximum southern solar declination.
- Illumination asymmetry is reversed relative to June.

From a geometric perspective:

- The Sun’s apparent path reaches its opposite extreme.
- One hemisphere experiences minimal daylight,
  while the other experiences maximal daylight.

South represents:
- **maximum contraction of solar declination**,
- minimum illumination in one hemisphere and maximum in the other,
- accumulation and latent potential within the cycle.

---

## Interaction with Other Cycles

The solar tropical cycle interacts strongly with:

- **Diurnal Cycle**  
  (local daylight length and solar altitude)

- **Solar Anomalistic Cycle**  
  (Earth–Sun distance modulation)

- **Lunar Cycles**  
  (seasonal modulation of eclipses and tides)

For example:
- the diurnal arc changes shape across the tropical year,
- eclipse geometry and visibility vary by season and latitude.

---

## Practical Applications

The solar tropical wheel is useful for:

- understanding seasonal geometry independent of hemisphere,
- visualizing equinoxes and solstices,
- comparing illumination patterns across latitudes,
- educational explanation of Earth’s axial tilt,
- contextualizing climatic and ecological rhythms.

---

## Conceptual Role in ChronoCompass

The tropical cycle defines the **illumination backbone** of the calendar year.

It answers the question:
> *How is Earth geometrically oriented relative to the Sun right now?*

Unlike the anomalistic cycle, it is:
- not about distance,
- not about orbital speed,
- but about **orientation, declination, and light distribution**.

---

## Summary

- The solar tropical cycle governs **solar declination and illumination geometry**.
- East and West correspond to **March and September equinoxes**.
- North and South correspond to **June and December solstices**.
- Houses represent declination states, not climatic seasons.
- The cycle is fully hemisphere-neutral.

ChronoCompass treats the solar tropical cycle as the **seasonal reference wheel** within the unified compass model.
